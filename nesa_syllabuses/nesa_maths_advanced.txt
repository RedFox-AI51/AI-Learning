NESA Stage 6 — Mathematics Advanced (2017)
NSW Syllabus for the Australian Curriculum
============================================================

RATIONALE
Mathematics Advanced develops students' ability to work with functions, calculus, financial mathematics, statistics and probability. The course builds on Stage 5 mathematics and provides the foundation for further study in mathematics and quantitative fields including physics, chemistry, medicine, engineering, economics, finance and statistics.

AIM
The study of Mathematics Advanced in Stage 6 aims to enable students to develop understanding and fluency in mathematics through inquiry, connecting mathematical concepts to their application, and building reasoning and communication skills for a range of contexts.

OUTCOMES
Year 11:
- MA11-1: uses algebraic and graphical techniques to solve, and where appropriate, compare alternative solutions to problems
- MA11-2: uses the concepts of functions and relations to model, analyse and solve practical problems
- MA11-3: uses the concepts and techniques of trigonometry in the solution of equations and problems involving geometric shapes
- MA11-4: uses the concepts and techniques of periodic functions in the solutions of trigonometric equations or proof of trigonometric identities
- MA11-5: interprets the meaning of the derivative, determines the derivative of functions and applies these to solve simple practical problems
- MA11-6: manipulates and solves expressions using the logarithmic and index laws, and uses logarithms and exponential functions to solve practical problems
- MA11-7: uses concepts and techniques from probability to present and interpret data and solve problems in a variety of contexts, including the use of probability distributions
- MA11-8: uses appropriate technology to investigate, organise, model and interpret information in a range of contexts
- MA11-9: provides reasoning to support conclusions which are appropriate to the context

Year 12:
- MA12-1: uses detailed algebraic and graphical techniques to critically construct, model and evaluate arguments in a range of familiar and unfamiliar contexts
- MA12-2: models and solves problems and makes informed decisions about financial situations using mathematical reasoning and techniques
- MA12-3: applies calculus techniques to model and solve problems
- MA12-4: applies the concepts and techniques of arithmetic and geometric sequences and series in the solution of problems
- MA12-5: applies the concepts and techniques of periodic functions in the solution of problems involving trigonometric graphs
- MA12-6: applies appropriate differentiation methods to solve problems
- MA12-7: applies the concepts and techniques of indefinite and definite integrals in the solution of problems
- MA12-8: solves problems using appropriate statistical processes
- MA12-9: chooses and uses appropriate technology effectively in a range of contexts
- MA12-10: constructs arguments to prove and justify results and provides reasoning to support conclusions which are appropriate to the context

--- Topic: Functions (Year 11) ---

Topic Focus: Functions involves the use of algebraic and graphical conventions and terminology to describe, interpret and model relationships between changing quantities. Knowledge of functions enables students to discover and generalise connections between algebraic and graphical representations and to describe interactions through dependent and independent variables.

MA-F1 WORKING WITH FUNCTIONS

F1.1 Algebraic techniques
Students:
- use index laws and surds
- solve quadratic equations using the quadratic formula and by completing the square
- manipulate complex algebraic expressions involving algebraic fractions

F1.2 Introduction to functions
Students:
- define and use a function and a relation as mappings between sets, and as a rule or formula that defines one variable in terms of another
- define a relation as any set of ordered pairs (x, y) of real numbers
- understand the formal definition of a function as a set of ordered pairs (x, y) such that no two ordered pairs have the same first component
- use function notation, domain and range, independent and dependent variables
- understand and use interval notation, e.g. [4, ∞)
- understand the concept of the graph of a function
- identify types of functions and relations on a given domain, knowing what is meant by one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-one and many-to-many
- use the vertical line test to identify a function
- determine if a function is one-to-one
- define odd and even functions algebraically and recognise their geometric properties
- define the sum, difference, product and quotient of functions and consider their domains and ranges
- define and use the composite function f(g(x)) where appropriate and identify the domain and range
- recognise that solving f(x) = 0 corresponds to finding x-intercepts of the graph of y = f(x)

F1.3 Linear, quadratic and cubic functions
Students:
- model, analyse and solve problems involving linear functions
- recognise that direct variation produces a straight-line graph
- explain the geometrical significance of m and c in f(x) = mx + c
- derive the equation of a straight line through a fixed point (x₁, y₁) with gradient m using y - y₁ = m(x - x₁)
- derive the equation of a straight line through two points using m = (y₂ - y₁)/(x₂ - x₁)
- understand that parallel lines have the same gradient and that lines with gradients m₁ and m₂ are perpendicular if and only if m₁m₂ = -1
- find equations of straight lines including parallel and perpendicular lines
- model, analyse and solve problems involving quadratic functions
- recognise features of a quadratic graph including its parabolic nature, turning point, axis of symmetry and intercepts
- find the vertex and intercepts of a quadratic graph by factorising, completing the square, or solving the quadratic equation
- understand the role of the discriminant in relation to the position of the graph
- find the equation of a quadratic given sufficient information
- solve practical problems involving simultaneous linear and/or quadratic functions algebraically and graphically, including determining the break-even point of a simple business problem
- recognise cubic functions of the form f(x) = kx³, f(x) = k(x-b)³+c and f(x) = k(x-a)(x-b)(x-c) from their equation and/or graph and identify important features

F1.4 Further functions and relations
Students:
- define a real polynomial P(x) = aₙxⁿ + aₙ₋₁xⁿ⁻¹ + ... + a₁x + a₀ and identify its coefficients and degree
- identify the shape and features of graphs of polynomial functions in factored form and sketch their graphs
- recognise that f(x) = k/x represents inverse variation, identify the hyperbolic shape and its asymptotes
- define the absolute value |x| as the distance from the origin on a number line
- use and apply the notation |x| and graph y = |x|; recognise and sketch y = |ax + b|
- solve simple absolute value equations |ax + b| = k algebraically and graphically
- given the graph of y = f(x), sketch y = -f(x), y = f(-x) and y = -f(-x) using reflections
- recognise features of x² + y² = r² and (x-a)² + (y-b)² = r², including their circular shapes, centres and radii
- derive the equation of a circle from Pythagoras' theorem; recognise that a circle is not a function
- transform equations of the form x² + y² + ax + by + c = 0 into (x-a)² + (y-b)² = r² by completing the square
- sketch circles and find their equations from graphs
- recognise that y = √(r² - x²) and y = -√(r² - x²) are functions with semicircular graphs


--- Topic: Trigonometric Functions (Year 11) ---

Topic Focus: Trigonometric Functions involves the study of periodic functions in geometric, algebraic, numerical and graphical representations. Knowledge of trigonometric functions enables solving of practical problems involving triangles or periodic graphs such as waves and signals.

MA-T1 TRIGONOMETRY AND MEASURE OF ANGLES

T1.1 Trigonometry
Students:
- use sine, cosine and tangent ratios to solve problems involving right-angled triangles where angles are measured in degrees, or degrees and minutes
- establish and use the sine rule, cosine rule and area of a triangle formula for solving problems
- find angles and sides involving the ambiguous case of the sine rule
- solve problems involving trigonometry in two and three dimensions
- interpret information about two- or three-dimensional contexts and construct diagrams where required
- solve practical problems involving Pythagoras' theorem and trigonometry of triangles, including angles of elevation and depression, true bearings and compass bearings in navigation

T1.2 Radians
Students:
- understand the unit circle definition of sinθ, cosθ and tanθ and periodicity using degrees
- sketch the trigonometric functions in degrees for 0° ≤ x ≤ 360°
- define and use radian measure and understand its relationship with degree measure
- convert between degrees and radians using 360° = 2π radians
- recognise and use the exact values of sinθ, cosθ and tanθ in both degrees and radians for integer multiples of π/6 and π/4
- understand the unit circle definition of sinθ, cosθ and tanθ and periodicity using radians
- solve problems involving trigonometric ratios of angles of any magnitude in both degrees and radians
- recognise and sketch graphs of y = sin x, y = cos x and y = tan x on extended domains in degrees and radians
- derive and use the formula for arc length l = rθ and for the area of a sector A = ½r²θ
- solve problems involving sector areas, arc lengths and combinations of areas or lengths

MA-T2 TRIGONOMETRIC FUNCTIONS AND IDENTITIES
Students:
- define the reciprocal trigonometric functions y = cosec x, y = sec x and y = cot x
- sketch the graphs of reciprocal trigonometric functions in both radians and degrees
- prove and apply the Pythagorean identities: cos²x + sin²x = 1, 1 + tan²x = sec²x, and 1 + cot²x = cosec²x
- know the difference between an equation and an identity
- use tan x = sin x/cos x provided cos x ≠ 0
- prove trigonometric identities
- evaluate trigonometric expressions using angles of any magnitude and complementary angle results
- simplify trigonometric expressions and solve trigonometric equations, including those that reduce to quadratic equations


--- Topic: Calculus (Year 11) ---

Topic Focus: Calculus is concerned with how quantities change and provides a framework for developing quantitative models of change. This topic develops the basic concepts of differential calculus, including the connection between the gradient of the tangent to a curve and the instantaneous rate of change of a function.

MA-C1 INTRODUCTION TO DIFFERENTIATION

C1.1 Gradients of tangents
Students:
- distinguish between continuous and discontinuous functions and identify key elements of each type
- sketch graphs of continuous functions and compare with graphs of functions that have discontinuities
- describe the gradient of a secant through two nearby points as an approximation of the gradient of the tangent, improving in accuracy as the distance between the two points decreases
- examine and use the relationship between the angle of inclination θ of a line or tangent and the gradient m, establishing that tan θ = m

C1.2 Difference quotients
Students:
- describe the behaviour of a function and its tangent at a point using language including increasing, decreasing, constant, stationary, increasing at an increasing rate
- interpret and use the difference quotient [f(x+h) - f(x)]/h as the average rate of change of f(x) or the gradient of a chord or secant
- interpret the meaning of the gradient of a function in a variety of contexts, for example on distance-time or velocity-time graphs

C1.3 The derivative function and its graph
Students:
- examine the behaviour of the difference quotient as h → 0 as an informal introduction to the concept of a limit
- interpret the derivative as the gradient of the tangent to the graph of y = f(x) at a point x
- estimate numerically the value of the derivative at a point for simple power functions
- define the derivative f'(x) from first principles as lim[h→0] [f(x+h) - f(x)]/h and use the notation dy/dx = f'(x) = y'
- use first principles to find the derivative of simple polynomials up to and including degree 3
- understand the concept of the derivative as a function
- sketch the derivative function for a given graph without the use of algebraic techniques
- establish that f'(x) = 0 at a stationary point, f'(x) > 0 when increasing and f'(x) < 0 when decreasing
- identify families of curves with the same derivative function
- interpret and use the derivative at a point as the instantaneous rate of change of a function

C1.4 Calculating with derivatives
Students:
- use the formula d/dx(xⁿ) = nxⁿ⁻¹ for all real values of n
- differentiate a constant multiple of a function and the sum or difference of two functions
- apply the product rule: if h(x) = f(x)g(x) then h'(x) = f(x)g'(x) + f'(x)g(x)
- apply the quotient rule: if h(x) = f(x)/g(x) then h'(x) = [g(x)f'(x) - f(x)g'(x)] / [g(x)]²
- apply the chain rule: if h(x) = f(g(x)) then h'(x) = f'(g(x))g'(x), or dy/dx = (dy/du)(du/dx)
- calculate derivatives of power functions to solve problems including finding instantaneous rates of change
- use the derivative to find the equation of a tangent or normal to a graph at a given point
- determine the velocity of a particle given its displacement as a function of time
- determine the acceleration of a particle given its velocity as a function of time


--- Topic: Exponential and Logarithmic Functions (Year 11) ---

Topic Focus: Introduces exponential and logarithmic functions and develops their properties. The exponential function eˣ is introduced by considering graphs of the derivative of exponential functions. Applications include exponential growth and decay, population growth and compound interest.

MA-E1 LOGARITHMS AND EXPONENTIALS

E1.1 Introducing logarithms
Students:
- define logarithms as indices: y = aˣ is equivalent to x = log_a(y), defined only for a > 0, a ≠ 1
- recognise and sketch the graphs of y = kaˣ, y = ka⁻ˣ and y = log_a(x)
- recognise and use the inverse relationship between logarithms and exponentials
- understand and use that log_a(aˣ) = x for all real x, and a^(log_a(x)) = x for all x > 0

E1.2 Logarithmic laws and applications
Students:
- derive the logarithmic laws from the index laws and use them to simplify and evaluate expressions:
  log_a(m) + log_a(n) = log_a(mn)
  log_a(m) - log_a(n) = log_a(m/n)
  log_a(mⁿ) = n log_a(m)
  log_a(a) = 1, log_a(1) = 0, log_a(1/x) = -log_a(x)
- prove and use the change of base law: log_a(x) = log_b(x) / log_b(a)
- interpret and use logarithmic scales, for example decibels in acoustics, earthquake magnitude, octaves in music, pH in chemistry
- solve algebraic, graphical and numerical problems involving logarithms in a variety of practical and abstract contexts

E1.3 The exponential function and natural logarithms
Students:
- establish and use the formula d/dx(eˣ) = eˣ
- using technology, sketch and explore the gradient function of exponential functions and determine that there is a unique number e ≈ 2.71828, for which d/dx(eˣ) = eˣ
- apply differentiation rules to functions involving the exponential function f(x) = keᵃˣ
- define the natural logarithm ln x = log_e(x) from the exponential function f(x) = eˣ
- recognise and use the inverse relationship of y = eˣ and y = ln x
- use ln x and the relationships e^(ln x) = x for x > 0 and ln(eˣ) = x for all real x
- use the logarithmic laws to simplify and evaluate natural logarithmic expressions and solve equations

E1.4 Graphs and applications
Students:
- solve equations involving indices using logarithms
- graph an exponential function y = aˣ for a > 0 and its transformations y = kaˣ + c and y = kaˣ⁺ᵇ
- interpret the meaning of the intercepts of an exponential graph
- establish and use the algebraic properties of exponential functions
- solve problems involving exponential functions in practical and abstract contexts
- graph a logarithmic function y = log_a(x) for a > 0 and its transformations y = k log_a(x) + c
- recognise that y = aˣ and y = log_a(x) are reflections in the line y = x
- model situations and solve equations involving logarithmic or exponential functions
- identify contexts suitable for modelling by exponential and logarithmic functions and use these to solve practical problems


--- Topic: Statistical Analysis (Year 11) ---

Topic Focus: Statistical Analysis involves the exploration, display, analysis and interpretation of data to identify and communicate key information. The topic introduces conditional probability, independence, and discrete random variables and their use in modelling random processes involving chance.

MA-S1 PROBABILITY AND DISCRETE PROBABILITY DISTRIBUTIONS

S1.1 Probability and Venn diagrams
Students:
- understand and use the concepts and language associated with theoretical probability, relative frequency and the probability scale
- solve problems involving simulations or trials of experiments in a variety of contexts
- identify factors that could complicate the simulation of real-world events
- use relative frequencies obtained from data as point estimates of probabilities
- use arrays and tree diagrams to determine the outcomes and probabilities for multi-stage experiments
- use Venn diagrams, set language and notation for events, including A' (complement), A∩B (intersection) and A∪B (union), and recognise mutually exclusive events
- establish and use the rules: P(A') = 1 - P(A) and P(A∪B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A∩B)
- understand the notion of conditional probability and recognise language that indicates conditionality
- use the notation P(A|B) and the formula P(A|B) = P(A∩B)/P(B), P(B) ≠ 0
- understand the notion of independence of an event A from an event B, as defined by P(A|B) = P(A)
- use the multiplication law P(A∩B) = P(A)P(B) for independent events A and B

S1.2 Discrete probability distributions
Students:
- define and categorise random variables; know the difference between discrete and continuous random variables
- use discrete random variables and associated probabilities to solve practical problems
- use relative frequencies obtained from data to obtain point estimates of probabilities associated with a discrete random variable
- recognise uniform discrete random variables and use them to model random phenomena with equally likely outcomes
- examine simple examples of non-uniform discrete random variables, and recognise that the sum of the probabilities is 1
- recognise the mean or expected value E(X) = μ of a discrete random variable X as a measure of centre and evaluate it in simple cases
- recognise the variance Var(X) and standard deviation σ of a discrete random variable as measures of spread and evaluate them in simple cases
- use Var(X) = E(X - μ)² = E(X²) - μ² for a random variable
- understand that a sample mean x̄ is an estimate of the population mean μ, and the sample standard deviation s is an estimate of the population standard deviation σ, improving as sample size increases

MA-F2 GRAPHING TECHNIQUES

- use graphical methods with supporting algebraic working to solve practical problems involving any of the functions within the scope of this syllabus
- select and use an appropriate method to graph a given function, including finding intercepts, considering the sign of f(x) and using symmetry
- determine asymptotes and discontinuities where appropriate (vertical and horizontal asymptotes only)
- determine the number of solutions of an equation by considering appropriate graphs
- solve linear and quadratic inequalities by sketching appropriate graphs


--- Topic: Trigonometric Functions (Year 12) ---

MA-T3 TRIGONOMETRIC FUNCTIONS AND GRAPHS

--- Topic: Calculus (Year 12) ---

Topic Focus: Calculus Year 12 involves the study of both differentiation and integration, developing students' capacity to operate with and model situations involving change.

MA-C2 DIFFERENTIAL CALCULUS

C2.1 Differentiation of trigonometric, exponential and logarithmic functions
Students:
- establish d/dx(sin x) = cos x and d/dx(cos x) = -sin x and calculate derivatives of trigonometric functions
- establish and use the formula d/dx(aˣ) = (ln a)aˣ
- calculate the derivative of the natural logarithm function d/dx(ln x) = 1/x
- establish and use the formula d/dx(log_a(x)) = 1/(x ln a)

C2.2 Rules of differentiation
Students:
- apply the product, quotient and chain rules to differentiate functions such as xe^x, tan x, 1/xⁿ, x sin x, e⁻ˣ sin x and f(ax+b)
- use the chain rule to establish d/dx(e^(f(x))) = f'(x)e^(f(x))
- use the chain rule to establish d/dx(ln(f(x))) = f'(x)/f(x)
- use logarithmic laws to simplify an expression before differentiating
- use the chain rule to establish and use the derivatives of sin(f(x)), cos(f(x)) and tan(f(x))

MA-C3 APPLICATIONS OF DIFFERENTIATION

C3.1 The first and second derivatives
Students:
- use the first derivative to investigate the shape of the graph of a function
- deduce from the sign of the first derivative whether a function is increasing, decreasing or stationary
- use the first derivative to find intervals over which a function is increasing or decreasing, and where its stationary points are located
- use the first derivative to investigate stationary points and classify them as local maximum, local minimum or neither
- determine the greatest or least value of a function over a given domain and distinguish between local and global minima and maxima
- define and interpret the concept of the second derivative as the rate of change of the first derivative, for example recognise acceleration as the second derivative of displacement with respect to time
- understand the concepts of concavity and points of inflection and their relationship with the second derivative
- use the second derivative to determine concavity and the nature of stationary points
- understand that when the second derivative equals 0 this does not necessarily represent a point of inflection

C3.2 Applications of the derivative
Students:
- use any functions covered in the scope of this syllabus and their derivatives to solve practical and abstract problems
- use calculus to determine stationary points, find local and global maxima and minima and points of inflection, examine behaviour of a function as x → ±∞ and sketch the graph
- solve optimisation problems in contexts including displacement, velocity, acceleration, area, volume, business, finance, growth and decay
- define variables and construct functions to represent relationships between variables related to contexts involving optimisation
- use calculus to establish the location of local and global maxima and minima, including checking endpoints if required
- evaluate solutions and their reasonableness given the constraints of the domain

MA-C4 INTEGRAL CALCULUS

C4.1 The anti-derivative
Students:
- define anti-differentiation as the reverse of differentiation and use the notation ∫f(x)dx for anti-derivatives or indefinite integrals
- recognise that any two anti-derivatives of f(x) differ by a constant
- establish and use ∫xⁿ dx = (1/(n+1))xⁿ⁺¹ + c, for n ≠ -1
- establish and use ∫[f'(x)][f(x)]ⁿ dx = (1/(n+1))[f(x)]ⁿ⁺¹ + c where n ≠ -1 (reverse chain rule)
- establish and use the formulae for the anti-derivatives of sin(ax+b), cos(ax+b) and sec²(ax+b)
- establish and use ∫eˣ dx = eˣ + c and ∫e^(ax+b) dx = (1/a)e^(ax+b) + c
- establish and use ∫(1/x) dx = ln|x| + c and ∫f'(x)/f(x) dx = ln|f(x)| + c
- establish and use ∫aˣ dx = aˣ/(ln a) + c
- recognise and use linearity of anti-differentiation
- examine families of anti-derivatives of a given function graphically
- determine indefinite integrals of the form ∫f(ax+b) dx
- determine f(x), given f'(x) and an initial condition f(a) = b in practical and abstract applications

C4.2 Areas and the definite integral
Students:
- know that the area under a curve refers to the area between a function and the x-axis, bounded by two values of the independent variable, and interpret this in a variety of contexts
- determine the approximate area under a curve using squares, rectangles (inner and outer), triangles or trapezia
- use the notation of the definite integral ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx for the area under the curve y = f(x) from x = a to x = b if f(x) ≥ 0
- use the Trapezoidal rule to estimate areas under curves
- use geometric arguments to approximate ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx using n trapezia with equal widths:
  ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx ≈ (b-a)/(2n) [f(a) + f(b) + 2{f(x₁) + ... + f(xₙ₋₁)}]
- understand the relationship of position to signed areas: signed area above the horizontal axis is positive, below is negative
- use the formula ∫ₐᵇ f(x)dx = F(b) - F(a), where F(x) is the anti-derivative of f(x), to calculate definite integrals
- understand and use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus: F'(x) = d/dx[∫ₐˣ f(t)dt] = f(x)
- use symmetry properties of even and odd functions to simplify calculations of area
- recognise and use the additivity and linearity of definite integrals
- calculate total change by integrating instantaneous rate of change
- calculate the area under a curve and areas between curves determined by any functions within the scope of this syllabus


--- Topic: Financial Mathematics (Year 12) ---

Topic Focus: Financial Mathematics involves sequences and series and their application to financial situations, including annuities, compound interest, and loans.

MA-M1 MODELLING FINANCIAL SITUATIONS

M1.1 Modelling investments and loans
Students:
- solve compound interest problems involving financial decisions including a home loan, savings account, car loan or superannuation
- identify an annuity as an investment account with regular equal contributions and interest compounding at the end of each period, or a single-sum investment from which regular equal withdrawals are made
- use technology to model an annuity as a recurrence relation and investigate the effect of varying the interest rate or the amount and frequency of contributions or withdrawals
- use a table of interest factors to perform annuity calculations, e.g. calculating the present or future value of an annuity

M1.2 Arithmetic sequences and series
Students:
- know the difference between a sequence and a series
- recognise and use the recursive definition of an arithmetic sequence: Tₙ = Tₙ₋₁ + d, T₁ = a
- establish and use the formula for the nth term of an arithmetic sequence: Tₙ = a + (n-1)d, recognising its linear nature
- establish and use the formulae for the sum of the first n terms: Sₙ = (n/2)(a + l) and Sₙ = (n/2)[2a + (n-1)d]
- identify and use arithmetic sequences and series in contexts involving discrete linear growth or decay such as simple interest

M1.3 Geometric sequences and series
Students:
- recognise and use the recursive definition of a geometric sequence: Tₙ = rTₙ₋₁, T₁ = a
- establish and use the formula for the nth term of a geometric sequence: Tₙ = arⁿ⁻¹, recognising its exponential nature
- establish and use the formula for the sum of the first n terms: Sₙ = a(1 - rⁿ)/(1 - r) = a(rⁿ - 1)/(r - 1)
- derive and use the formula for the limiting sum of a geometric series with |r| < 1: S = a/(1 - r)
- understand the limiting behaviour as n → ∞ and its application to a geometric series

M1.4 Financial applications of sequences and series
Students:
- use geometric sequences to model and analyse practical problems involving exponential growth and decay
- calculate the effective annual rate of interest and use results to compare investment returns and costs of loans when interest is paid or charged daily, monthly, quarterly or six-monthly
- solve problems involving compound interest loans or investments, including determining the future value of an investment, the number of compounding periods for an investment to exceed a given value, and the interest rate needed
- recognise a reducing balance loan as a compound interest loan with periodic repayments and solve related problems
- solve problems involving financial decisions including a home loan, savings account, car loan or superannuation
- calculate the future value or present value of an annuity using the sum of the calculated compounded values of each contribution and the formula for the sum of the first n terms of a geometric sequence


--- Topic: Statistical Analysis (Year 12) ---

Topic Focus: Statistical Analysis involves the exploration, display, analysis and interpretation of data. Year 12 covers descriptive statistics and bivariate data analysis, and introduces continuous random variables and the normal distribution.

MA-S2 DESCRIPTIVE STATISTICS AND BIVARIATE DATA ANALYSIS

S2.1 Data (grouped and ungrouped) and summary statistics
Students:
- classify data relating to a single random variable
- organise, interpret and display data into appropriate tabular and/or graphical representations including Pareto charts, cumulative frequency distribution tables or graphs, parallel box-plots and two-way tables
- compare the suitability of different methods of data presentation in real-world contexts
- summarise and interpret grouped and ungrouped data through appropriate graphs and summary statistics
- calculate measures of central tendency and spread and investigate their suitability in real-world contexts
- investigate real-world examples from the media illustrating appropriate and inappropriate uses of measures of central tendency and spread
- identify outliers and investigate and describe their effect on summary statistics
- use approaches for identifying outliers, including the criteria below Q1 - 1.5 × IQR and above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR

S2.2 Bivariate data analysis
Students:
- construct a bivariate scatterplot to identify patterns in the data that suggest the presence of an association
- describe patterns, features and associations of bivariate datasets: form (linear/non-linear), and for linear: direction (positive/negative) and strength (strong/moderate/weak)
- identify the dependent and independent variables within bivariate datasets where appropriate
- calculate and interpret Pearson's correlation coefficient (r) using technology to quantify the strength of a linear association
- model a linear relationship by fitting an appropriate line of best fit to a scatterplot
- fit a line of best fit to the data by eye and using technology; fit a least-squares regression line using technology
- interpret the intercept and gradient of the fitted line
- use the appropriate line of best fit to make predictions by interpolation or extrapolation
- distinguish between interpolation and extrapolation and recognise the limitations of using the fitted line to make predictions
- demonstrate an awareness of issues of privacy and bias, ethics, and responsiveness to diverse groups and cultures when collecting and using data

MA-S3 RANDOM VARIABLES

S3.1 Continuous random variables
Students:
- use relative frequencies and histograms obtained from data to estimate probabilities associated with a continuous random variable
- understand and use the concepts of a probability density function of a continuous random variable
- know the two properties of a probability density function: f(x) ≥ 0 for all real x, and ∫₋∞^∞ f(x)dx = 1
- define the probability as the area under the graph of the probability density function: P(X ≤ r) = ∫ₐʳ f(x)dx

S3.2 The normal distribution
Students:
- identify the numerical and graphical properties of normally distributed data
- calculate probabilities and quantiles associated with a given normal distribution using technology and use these to solve practical problems
- identify contexts suitable for modelling by normal random variables, e.g. the height of a group of students
- recognise features of the graph of the probability density function of the normal distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ
- visually represent probabilities by shading areas under the normal curve
- understand and calculate the z-score (standardised score): z = (x - μ)/σ
- describe the z-score as the number of standard deviations a value lies above or below the mean
- use z-scores to compare scores from different datasets, for example comparing students' subject examination scores
- apply the empirical rule: approximately 68% of data has z-scores between -1 and 1, 95% between -2 and 2, and 99.7% between -3 and 3
- use z-scores to identify probabilities of events less or more extreme than a given event
- use statistical tables and technology to determine probabilities
- use z-scores to make judgements related to outcomes of a given event or sets of data


============================================================
KEY GLOSSARY TERMS
============================================================

absolute value: The absolute value |x| of a real number x is its magnitude without regard to its sign; the distance from the origin on a number line.
amplitude: The height from the horizontal centre line to the peak of the graph of a function.
annuity: A compound interest investment from which payments are made or received on a regular basis for a fixed period of time.
anti-derivative: A function F(x) whose derivative is f(x). The process of finding the anti-derivative is called integration.
arithmetic sequence: A sequence where the difference between any two successive members is constant.
asymptote: A line that a function approaches but never reaches.
concavity: A function is concave up on an interval if the tangent lies below the curve; concave down if the tangent lies above the curve.
conditional probability: The probability P(A|B) that event A occurs given that event B has occurred.
continuous random variable: A numerical variable that can take any value along a continuum.
derivative: The gradient of the tangent to the curve y = f(x) at a given point; the instantaneous rate of change.
discrete random variable: A numerical variable whose values can be listed.
empirical rule: For normally distributed data, approximately 68% of data has z-scores between -1 and 1, 95% between -2 and 2, and 99.7% between -3 and 3.
expected value: The mean E(X) of a random variable X; a measure of the central tendency of its distribution.
geometric sequence: A sequence where each term is found by multiplying the previous one by a fixed common ratio.
instantaneous rate of change: The rate of change at a particular moment; the value of the derivative at that point.
least-squares regression line: The line that minimises the sum of the squares of the residuals; the line of best fit.
normal distribution: A continuous distribution whose graph is bell-shaped and symmetric about the mean, with mean = median = mode.
Pearson's correlation coefficient: A statistic r that measures the strength of the linear relationship between two variables; value lies between -1 and 1.
point of inflection: A point on a curve where the tangent exists and crosses the curve; where concavity changes.
stationary point: A point on the graph y = f(x) where f'(x) = 0.
z-score: A statistical measurement of how many standard deviations a raw score is above or below the mean; z = (x - μ)/σ.
